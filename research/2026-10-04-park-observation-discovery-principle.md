# PARK OBSERVATION → DISCOVERY PRINCIPLE
Date: 2026-10-04
Type: Field observation / principle extraction / experiment design
Status: Principle candidate → ready for minimum experiment

## 0. Why this log exists

This document preserves the reasoning process from a park observation conversation, not just the conclusion.

The aim was to move from concrete observations — ants, a spider, a spider web, a fire truck, and hut play — toward a reusable principle:

> **What kind of situation makes a person want to discover something?**
>
> and more specifically:
>
> **What is the structure of discovering something that is hidden?**

The important point is that the object itself is not enough. The surrounding situation changes whether discovery becomes play.

---

## 1. Raw observations

### 1-1. Ants

Observation:
- Ants were interesting when they appeared in an unexpected place.
- The appeal was not simply “ants are interesting.”
- A small living thing appearing where attention had not originally been directed created a search impulse.

Initial note:
- **Unexpected location** may be part of discovery.
- The environment becomes a field containing “something that might still be there.”

### 1-2. Spider

Observation:
- The spider was fun to notice, but also slightly scary.
- The slight fear did not simply cancel interest.
- It increased caution and attention.

Initial note:
- Discovery may become stronger when there is a small amount of emotional tension.
- “I want to see it” and “I do not want to get too close” can coexist.

### 1-3. Spider web

Observation:
- The spider web was found incidentally.
- A web is not the spider itself; it is a **trace that suggests an unseen cause**.

Initial note:
- A trace can be more important than a directly visible object.
- The trace creates a question: “Is there a spider nearby?”
- This shifts discovery from recognition to inference.

### 1-4. Fire truck

Observation:
- The fire truck first existed as sound / movement / presence before full visual confirmation.
- Hearing it creates “Where is it?” and causes active looking.
- The target is moving, so the opportunity to find it may disappear.

Initial note:
- **Advance signal + temporary target + direction search** strongly creates discovery behavior.
- A target that may vanish produces urgency without needing a formal timer.

### 1-5. Hut play

Observation:
- The hut was repeatedly reinterpreted through play: earthquake, sleeping, “Mamadol” shop, etc.
- The child could decide what happened next.
- The same physical structure changed meaning according to the role and event.

Initial note:
- Fun did not come only from finding a pre-hidden object.
- It also came from treating the environment as containing **latent possibilities** and making the next event appear.
- Therefore discovery may include both:
  1. finding something hidden by the world;
  2. revealing a possibility hidden inside an ordinary object or place.

---

## 2. First hypotheses

From the observations, several candidate explanations appeared.

### Hypothesis A — Novelty alone

Maybe people search simply because something is new.

Problem:
- A totally unknown object with no clue can be ignored.
- Novelty explains surprise after discovery better than the urge to begin searching.

### Hypothesis B — Hiddenness alone

Maybe concealment itself creates fun.

Problem:
- If there is no reason to believe anything is there, concealment can become indistinguishable from emptiness.
- “Completely hidden” can produce no search at all.

### Hypothesis C — Reward alone

Maybe discovery works because finding something gives a reward.

Problem:
- The park observations often had no external reward.
- Looking for the fire truck or spider was already motivating before any prize.

### Hypothesis D — A small information gap

A stronger candidate:
- The person receives enough information to infer that “something exists,”
- but not enough to know exactly what / where it is.

This produces a gap that can be closed through action.

## 3. Candidate principles collected during the discussion

The following were treated as ingredients that can generate “I want to find it”:

- advance signal / omen
- partial exposure
- prediction violation
- camouflage
- trace
- movement
- disappearance
- unexpected location
- unknown identity
- mild fear
- rarity
- limited opportunity
- chain reaction
- another person’s reaction
- information asymmetry
- competition
- collection
- hypothesis testing

These are not separate games. They are possible ways to create the same deeper structure.

## 4. Structural convergence

The observations gradually converged on the following sequence:

### Basic discovery loop

1. **Existence is suggested**
2. **The object is not fully visible / understood**
3. **A clue is available**
4. **The player forms a prediction**
5. **The player acts to verify it**
6. **The world answers**
7. **The answer creates the next clue**

Compact form:

> **hint of existence → concealment → clue → prediction → search/action → reveal → next clue**

This was the turning point from “interesting objects in a park” to “a reusable discovery structure.”

## 5. Why each park observation fits the structure

| Observation | Hint of existence | Hidden part | Search action | Reveal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ant | tiny movement / unusual presence | location / where it came from | look closely, follow | ant / route / nest possibility |
| Spider | movement / shape | exact position, behavior | approach carefully, track | spider becomes identifiable |
| Spider web | web as trace | spider itself | inspect surrounding area | possible owner / direction / new trace |
| Fire truck | siren / engine sound | visual position | turn, move, scan | fire truck appears |
| Hut play | familiar structure + role cue | possible next meaning/event | declare, enter, act | hut becomes house/shop/disaster scene |

The hut case expands the principle:
- not all hidden things are physical objects;
- **a possible interpretation or event can also be hidden**.

## 6. Research cross-check

### 6-1. Information-gap theory of curiosity

George Loewenstein’s information-gap account proposes that curiosity arises when a person becomes aware of a gap between what they know and what they want to know.

Connection to the park:
- Fire truck sound: “Something is nearby, but where?”
- Spider web: “Something made this, but is it here?”
- Ant: “Why is it here / where is it going?”

Important implication:
- Zero information is weak.
- Complete information is also weak.
- Curiosity is often strongest when **some information reveals that a resolvable gap exists**.

Reference:
- Loewenstein, G. (1994). *The Psychology of Curiosity: A Review and Reinterpretation*. Psychological Bulletin, 116(1), 75–98.
- https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75

### 6-2. Curiosity as information-seeking

Kidd & Hayden review curiosity as a driver of information-seeking and learning, and note that uncertainty and partial knowledge are central to many curiosity tasks.

Connection to the park:
- Discovery is not merely a reward delivered at the end.
- The act of resolving uncertainty can itself motivate behavior.

Reference:
- Kidd, C. & Hayden, B. Y. (2015). *The Psychology and Neuroscience of Curiosity*. Neuron, 88(3), 449–460.
- https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.09.010

### 6-3. Occlusion / amodal completion

Visual-perception research shows that people perceptually infer whole objects from partially occluded information.

Connection to the park:
- A fragment, trace, edge, movement, or sound can be enough for the brain to construct a possible whole.
- This supports the idea that **partial visibility can be more generative than full visibility** because it gives the player something to complete.

References:
- Rauschenberger, R. & Yantis, S. (2001). *Masking unveils pre-amodal completion representation in visual search*. Nature, 410, 369–372.
- https://doi.org/10.1038/35066577

### Working interpretation

The research does not prove that every park example follows one universal law.
However, it supports the core mechanism:

> People are likely to seek information when they can infer that a missing piece exists and believe their own action can resolve it.

## 7. Refined principle

### Principle candidate: “Resolvable Concealment”

A discovery situation becomes attractive when:

1. **something is probably there**
2. **it is not fully accessible yet**
3. **there is a clue that allows a hypothesis**
4. **the player has an action that can test the hypothesis**
5. **the answer arrives clearly enough to update the next prediction**

Formula:

> **Discovery desire ≈ evidence of existence × uncertainty × actionability × feedback**

If any one approaches zero:
- no evidence → no reason to search
- no uncertainty → nothing to discover
- no actionability → only passive mystery
- no feedback → search feels arbitrary

## 8. “Hidden object” is only one special case

The conversation initially moved toward a hide-and-seek / finding-game structure.

But the stronger abstraction is:

> **Hide not only objects, but information, causes, routes, identities, meanings, events, or relationships.**

Examples derived from the park:

- Object hidden: spider behind a structure
- Cause hidden: web exists, spider is unknown
- Location hidden: fire truck can be heard but not seen
- Route hidden: follow ants to discover where they go
- Meaning hidden: ordinary hut becomes a shop / house / earthquake scene
- Future hidden: something may appear only for a short time

This makes the principle usable far beyond literal hide-and-seek.

## 9. Minimal experiments

The goal is not to make a full game first.
Test whether the principle itself produces behavior.

### Experiment 1 — Visibility test

Create three tiny scenes with the same target.

A. Target fully visible  
B. Target 70–90% hidden but a clue is visible  
C. Target completely hidden with no clue

Measure:
- whether the child starts searching without instruction
- time until first search action
- time until discovery
- whether they ask to do it again

Prediction:
- B should create the strongest spontaneous search.

### Experiment 2 — Trace-before-object

Do not show the creature.
Only show a trace first.

Examples:
- web
- footprints
- moving leaf
- shadow
- sound

Measure:
- whether the player forms a hypothesis
- whether they move toward the source
- whether the discovery feels stronger than seeing the object immediately

### Experiment 3 — Moving opportunity

Use a sound source / moving target that can leave.

Structure:
- hear it first
- direction is ambiguous
- the target appears briefly
- it moves away

Measure:
- urgency
- directional search
- repeat attempts
- excitement after successful discovery

This directly translates the fire-truck observation.

### Experiment 4 — Hidden meaning

Use one simple 3D hut.

Do not add many assets.
Allow one cue to reinterpret it:
- smoke → “something happened”
- bell → “shop opened”
- shaking → “earthquake”
- sleeping sound → “someone is inside”

Measure:
- whether players invent the next role/event themselves
- whether they reuse the same object under a new interpretation

This tests whether “discovery” can operate on **meaning**, not only objects.

## 10. Minimum game structure for elementary-school children

To keep it simple and game-like:

1. A clue appears.
2. The player has a short window to find the source.
3. Finding it creates points / a new clue / a race against another player.
4. Sometimes the clue is misleading or the target moves.
5. The next discovery changes the environment slightly.

Possible one-sentence form:

> **“Something is nearby. Find it before it disappears.”**

This preserves:
- discovery
- time pressure
- uncertainty
- competition
- accidental events

without requiring a complicated rule set.

---

## 11. What not to conclude yet

Do **not** freeze the principle too early.

Open questions:
- Is mild fear actually increasing discovery, or only attention?
- How much clue is optimal before mystery becomes obvious?
- Does movement matter independently from time pressure?
- Is accidental discovery more memorable than instructed search?
- Does social information (“someone else saw it”) increase search more than an environmental clue?
- Can a hidden relationship be as compelling as a hidden object for children?
- At what point does concealment become frustration?

These should be answered through small observation loops rather than theory alone.
